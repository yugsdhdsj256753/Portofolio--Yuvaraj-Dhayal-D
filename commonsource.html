MOSFET Common-Source Amplifier

Understanding the operation, gain, biasing, active loads, output resistance and source degeneration of one of the fundamental building blocks in CMOS analog design.

By Yuvaraj Dhayal D · Analog IC Design

Introduction

The common-source amplifier is one of the most fundamental building blocks in analog circuit design. It is widely used in CMOS amplifiers, operational amplifiers, OTAs, comparators and other analog integrated circuits.

The basic idea is simple: a small change in the MOSFET gate voltage produces a change in drain current, and the load converts that current variation into an output voltage.

Core idea A common-source amplifier converts an input voltage variation into an output voltage variation through the MOSFET's transconductance.

1. Basic Common-Source Configuration

In a common-source amplifier, the input is applied to the gate and the output is taken from the drain. The source is common to the input and output paths.

When the input voltage increases, the MOSFET gate-source voltage increases. This increases the drain current.

VOUT = VDD − ID RD

Therefore, an increase in input voltage produces a decrease in output voltage. The common-source amplifier consequently provides approximately 180° phase inversion.

2. Why MOSFET Biasing Is Important

For amplification, the MOSFET is normally biased in the saturation region. The DC operating point is often called the Q-point.

For an NMOS:

VDS ≥ VGS − VTH

Defining the overdrive voltage as:

VOV = VGS − VTH

the saturation condition becomes:

VDS ≥ VOV

Proper biasing allows the small input signal to produce approximately linear variations around the operating point.

3. MOSFET Transconductance

The transconductance, gm, describes how effectively the MOSFET converts a change in gate-source voltage into a change in drain current.

gm = ∂ID / ∂VGS

For the long-channel square-law model:

gm = 2ID / VOV

A larger gm generally provides a larger voltage gain for the same output resistance.

4. Voltage Gain

For a simple resistor-loaded common-source amplifier, ignoring transistor output resistance:

Av ≈ −gm RD

The negative sign represents the phase inversion.

For example, if:

gm = 2 mS RD = 10 kΩ

then:

Av ≈ −20 V/V

5. Effect of MOSFET Output Resistance

A real MOSFET has finite output resistance due to channel length modulation. Therefore, the output resistance of the amplifier is not simply RD.

Rout ≈ RD || ro

The voltage gain becomes:

Av ≈ −gm (RD || ro)

This leads to an important general relationship:

Analog design intuition Voltage gain can often be understood approximately as transconductance multiplied by output resistance.
Av ≈ −gm Rout

6. Common-Source Amplifier with an Active Load

In integrated circuits, large resistors can consume significant silicon area. MOSFETs can therefore be used as active loads.

VDD | PMOS | +------ VOUT | NMOS | GND

The output resistance is approximately:

Rout ≈ ron || rop

Hence the gain can be approximated as:

Av ≈ −gmn (ron || rop)

Active loads are particularly important in CMOS analog integrated circuits because they can provide high output resistance without requiring a physically large resistor.

7. Diode-Connected MOSFET Load

Another common configuration uses a MOSFET whose gate and drain are connected together. This is known as a diode-connected MOSFET.

rdiode ≈ 1 / gm

The resulting load resistance is relatively small, so the voltage gain is generally lower than that obtained with a high-resistance active load.

8. Source Degeneration

A resistor can be inserted between the source and ground. This technique is called source degeneration.

VDD | RD | +------ VOUT | NMOS | RS | GND ↑ VIN

Source degeneration introduces local negative feedback. When the drain current increases, the voltage across RS increases, which reduces the effective VGS variation.

Ignoring ro, the approximate voltage gain becomes:

Av ≈ −gm RD / (1 + gm RS)

9. Gain vs Linearity

Source degeneration reduces voltage gain, but it can improve linearity and make the gain less sensitive to transistor parameter variations.

Without degeneration With degeneration
Higher gain Lower gain
More nonlinear Improved linearity
Higher sensitivity to device parameters More predictable behavior
Less local feedback Negative feedback

This is an important analog design trade-off:

More degeneration → Lower gain + Better linearity

10. Frequency Response

At high frequencies, parasitic capacitances become important. The main capacitances include:

  • Cgs — gate-to-source capacitance
  • Cgd — gate-to-drain capacitance
  • Cdb — drain-to-body capacitance

The gate-to-drain capacitance is particularly important because of the Miller effect. The effective input capacitance can increase substantially when the amplifier has significant voltage gain.

Therefore, increasing gain does not always mean improving the overall amplifier. Gain, bandwidth and stability must be considered together.

11. Design Parameters to Check

  • DC operating point
  • Drain current
  • VGS and VDS
  • MOSFET operating region
  • Transconductance gm
  • Output resistance ro
  • Voltage gain
  • Output swing
  • Linearity
  • Power consumption
  • Bandwidth
  • Stability

12. Why the Common-Source Amplifier Matters

The common-source stage appears throughout analog IC design. Understanding it provides the foundation for analysing more complex circuits such as:

  • Differential amplifiers
  • Operational amplifiers
  • OTAs
  • Comparators
  • Cascode amplifiers
  • Current mirrors
  • Active filters
  • Multi-stage amplifiers

Many advanced CMOS analog circuits can ultimately be understood by breaking them down into transistor transconductance, output resistance and feedback mechanisms.

Key Takeaways

  • The common-source amplifier converts gate-voltage variations into drain-current variations.
  • A resistor or active load converts the current variation into an output-voltage variation.
  • The output is inverted relative to the input.
  • MOSFET biasing is essential for maintaining the desired operating region.
  • Higher gm generally increases gain.
  • Higher output resistance generally increases gain.
  • Active loads can provide high output resistance in CMOS integrated circuits.
  • Source degeneration introduces negative feedback and improves linearity at the cost of gain.
  • Frequency response is affected strongly by parasitic capacitances and the Miller effect.

Final Perspective

The common-source amplifier is one of the best circuits for developing analog design intuition. Rather than memorizing only the gain equation, it is more useful to understand the complete signal path:

VIN → VGS → ΔID → ΔVOUT

Once this relationship becomes intuitive, more advanced CMOS analog circuits such as differential pairs, OTAs, cascodes and operational amplifiers become much easier to analyse and design.
