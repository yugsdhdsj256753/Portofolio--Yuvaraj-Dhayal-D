Cascode Low-Noise Amplifier (LNA)

Understanding how a common-source input transistor, common-gate cascode device and inductive matching network amplify weak RF signals while balancing noise, gain and impedance matching.

Published By Yuvaraj Dhayal D · Analog / RF IC Design

1. Introduction

A low-noise amplifier (LNA) is an important first-stage block in many RF receivers. It amplifies weak signals from an antenna while adding as little noise as practical. Its noise performance matters because noise introduced near the receiver input can significantly affect the sensitivity of the complete receive chain.

LNA design involves trade-offs among gain, noise figure, input and output matching, linearity, stability, bandwidth and power consumption. The best design depends on the target frequency and receiver requirements.

2. Circuit operation

M1 — Common-source transistor

The RF input is applied to the gate of M1. Its transconductance converts a small input-voltage variation into a drain-current variation. Device sizing and bias current influence gain, noise and linearity.

M2 — Common-gate cascode

M2 is biased by Vbias. The cascode device helps keep the drain voltage of M1 relatively stable, reduces the Miller effect and improves reverse isolation. It also contributes to the overall gain and headroom requirements.

Ld — Drain load

The drain inductor acts as a frequency-dependent load. Together with device and load capacitances, it can form a resonant network around the target RF band and help provide voltage gain.

Lg and Ls — Input network

The gate and source inductors work with transistor parasitic capacitances to tune the input response. Source degeneration also contributes a real component to the input impedance, helping match the amplifier to its source.

3. Input impedance and resonance

In an inductively source-degenerated common-source input stage, the source inductor provides frequency-dependent feedback. In a simplified small-signal model, the real part of the input impedance is related to the product of angular frequency, source inductance and transistor transconductance. The gate inductor is then selected to help cancel the input capacitive reactance near the design frequency.

f₀ ≈ 1 / (2π√(LC))

This is the ideal LC resonance relation. The actual resonance and matching depend on transistor parasitics, inductor quality factor, interconnect, loading and the chosen process technology.

4. Important performance parameters

Gain (S21)

Forward transmission indicates how effectively the LNA transfers the input signal to the output. Gain must be assessed across the intended frequency band and under the correct source and load conditions.

Noise figure (NF)

Noise figure describes how much an amplifier degrades the signal-to-noise ratio. A lower noise figure is generally desirable, especially for the first stage of a receiver.

NF (dB) = 10 log₁₀(F), where F = SNRᵢₙ / SNRₒᵤₜ

Input matching (S11)

S11 indicates input reflection. Good matching near the operating frequency helps transfer available signal power from the antenna or preceding stage.

Linearity and stability

IIP3 and the 1-dB compression point help characterize distortion under larger input signals. Stability metrics such as Rollett's K factor or μ should be checked over a suitably broad frequency range.

Power consumption

Supply voltage and bias current set the DC power budget. Increasing current may improve some performance metrics, but usually increases power and can change linearity and noise behaviour.

5. A practical simulation workflow

  1. Define the target RF band, supply voltage, gain, noise figure, matching, linearity and power requirements.
  2. Choose the transistor dimensions and bias point, checking that the devices operate in the intended region.
  3. Design and tune Lg, Ls and Ld using the device small-signal model and parasitic capacitances.
  4. Simulate S-parameters, gain, noise figure, input/output matching and stability in the chosen RF simulator.
  5. Check process and temperature corners; if layout is available, run DRC, LVS, parasitic extraction and post-layout simulations.
